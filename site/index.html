She hesitated.
The final transcript
dropped the number.

Indian callers hesitate mid-sentence — matlab, the verbal equivalent of “umm”. The gap splits her turn. The partials carry all seven digits; the final that lands 105 ms later ends at matlab and reports that it covered the whole turn. One call reproduces it — the socket trace is below, message by message.

How often is a caller in a position to hit this? Measured, not assumed: in 1,788 unscripted Hindi telephone recordings, 42.6% carry a hesitation long enough to end the turn early at the documented default — and streaming twenty of them at that default, 10 of 20 had the turn ended inside the hesitation. And when the turn does end early, an agent replying at that moment has the account number in 0% of calls — while the session transcript has it.

Two things this is not: recognition was accurate throughout, and the endpointing gate is a documented setting doing what it is told. Neither is the finding — the dropped entity and what the agent is holding when it answers are.

On the two shorter utterances the transcript simply stops at the filler: “The reference is Matlab”, and the entity is gone rather than mangled.

04the measurement

The cliff sits just above the default.

Twelve utterances streamed in real time at each gate setting. Flat at 1.00 through every value up to and including the 500 ms default, then straight to zero. Moving the gate to 700 ms removes the effect entirely at this hesitation length.

And how often does a caller actually pause that long? Measured on 5.02 hours of spontaneous Hindi telephone speech: 17.9% of mid-utterance pauses exceed the 500 ms default — about one in six. At 700 ms it is one in seventeen.

“Raise the gate” is not advice on its own — the gate elapses before the agent may answer, so every millisecond added to it is added to every turn of every call. The decision needs both columns. The right-hand one is the share of recordings carrying at least one pause that long: a caller only has to hesitate once for the call to go wrong.

05after the interruption

One turn is not a conversation.

Being cut off is the first half of the failure. She keeps talking — the number is still coming — while the agent is already composing a reply to a question she had not finished. Three things then decide the call, and all three are timestamps we already hold.

The first two rows are the same call scored two ways, and the gap between them is the cost of answering at end-of-turn: the value is on the socket, just not yet. The fourth row is why she then has to repeat it into an agent that is talking — 800 ms is a generous figure for LLM-plus-TTS first audio, and the row does not rest on it: the earliest cut here lands 1,249 ms before she finishes, so any agent audible inside 1.25 s collides on all twelve. A slower stack collides less — it trades the collision for dead air.

3 of the 12 sessions are scored as abstained rather than failed: they are spoken dates in Devanagari digit words, which this scorer cannot parse. Calling that a vendor failure would be inventing a result, so the session row is a rate over what is readable. Nothing here needed new audio: the split-turn finals and the injected pause were already in the stored rows, which is what a conversation metric costs once the timing ground truth is exact.

06real callers

Real audio says something else.

Every number above rides on a synthesised caller, so the acoustics and the placement of the hesitation are ours. PIR does not need that: it needs only the moment speech ended, and no known entity — so real recordings can drive it directly. 20 unscripted Hindi telephone recordings that already carry a mid-utterance pause of 500 ms or more, streamed as they were recorded, at the default gate.

10 of the 20 real callers had a turn ended inside their own hesitation, at the documented default, with no synthesis anywhere in the path. These recordings were selected for carrying a pause that long, so that rate is conditional on it; composed with the 42.6% of recordings that do, it puts roughly 21.3% of this corpus's callers in the same position — about 1 in 5. The control says how much of the rest is the line rather than the endpointer: replacing the hesitation with digital silence and changing nothing else takes it to 70% — +20 points. At a median pause floor of -33.0 dB the live line's own noise protects some callers, but it is a secondary effect, not the explanation. The exposed caller is the one on the quiet handset, and noise suppression placed ahead of the VAD moves callers toward the cut, not away from it. The wider column is every early turn end, including the ordinary splitting of a long monologue, and it is reported only for completeness: on spontaneous voice messages that number is close to free.

The cost of using real audio is stated rather than hidden: true_end_ms is no longer known by construction but measured by the same energy VAD that found the pause — a 20 ms analysis frame, and a per-recording spread of 20 ms median, 300 ms worst when the threshold is halved and doubled. The VAD takes the last frame above threshold as the end, so a trailing quiet syllable makes an early cut look less early. Like the latency in the socket timestamps, the error runs against the finding.

08the generalisation

Take the microphone away.
The measure stays.

Silent failure is a property of agents, not of speech pipelines: when the input was corrupted, did the behaviour show the agent noticed? That question survives losing the audio. The same scoring function, pointed at a retrieval pipeline — authored records, an authored corruption, no microphone anywhere.

The last row is the control, and it is the reason the rest is worth reading. It is authored to be unnoticeable — one digit changed, still perfectly well-formed, nothing in the records marking it. If it scored the same as the detectable classes, the metric would be reading how often the agent confirms rather than whether it spotted an error. It does not.

Two results came out negative and are kept. 10the tool

Point it at your own agent.
